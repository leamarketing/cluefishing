Manifesto

Use the tool. Keep the credit honest.

AI has quietly become one of the best collaborators most of us have ever had. It can help build something faster, translate an idea, clean up a draft, or generate a texture nobody had time to paint by hand. That's worth being glad about, not defensive about.

The tool was never the problem. It starts the moment a person lets someone else believe an idea, a voice, or a piece of work was entirely theirs when a machine carried real weight in it.

We heard that failure mode get a name in a recorded conversation about AI ethics: clawfishing, presenting an AI-made creation or template as your own without disclosure. It's a good word for a real problem, so we built the mirror image of it. Cluefishing is the practice of noticing the tell, and of disclosing your own work before anyone has to go looking for it.

The idea still has to come from a person. AI can carry it beautifully. It shouldn't get silent credit for originating it.

— Geir Amdal, founder

Field guide

Three entries to start with

001  

The Clawfishing Problem

+
What the word means, and why the mirror image needed a name too.

Clawfishing is simple to define and easy to miss in the wild: an AI-generated creation, or something built from an AI template, presented as if it came entirely from the person posting it. No disclosure, no credit to the tool, no indication that a prompt did some of the work.

It's not the AI use that's the issue. It's the silence around it. A drawing, a paragraph, a song, or a product photo made with AI assistance is fine to share; it's the implied claim, I made this myself, unaided, that turns ordinary tool use into something closer to plagiarism.

Cluefishing starts on the other side of that line: reading for the tell, and building the habit of disclosing before anyone has to ask.

002  

Provenance Is Not Paranoia

+
Metadata, content credentials, and why "where did this come from" is a normal question now.

Photographers have used EXIF metadata for decades to prove when and how an image was captured. The same idea is now being extended to AI content through standards like the C2PA content credentials effort, an industry coalition (Adobe, Microsoft, the BBC and others are publicly involved) building a way to attach a verifiable "how this was made" record to a file.

None of this requires trusting a single company's word. It just means provenance, the trail showing how something was made, is becoming a normal thing to check, the way you'd check a source on a news story. Asking for it isn't an accusation. It's due diligence.

Practically: keep your prompts, your drafts, and your source files. If someone asks how a piece was made, the honest answer should already be sitting in a folder somewhere.

003  

Six Places the Tell Shows Up

+
What to actually look at before you file a report.

1. Missing metadata. A photo with no camera information, or a document with no edit history, isn't proof of anything by itself, but it's worth a second look.

2. Suspiciously even rhythm. Human writing wobbles: sentence length varies, tangents creep in. Text that marches at the same pace for paragraphs can be a sign, not a certainty.

3. Stock transitions. Phrases that show up in almost every AI-drafted piece, moreover, it's important to note, in today's fast-paced world, aren't wrong on their own, but three of them in one paragraph is a pattern.

4. Confident errors. Clean grammar paired with a factual mistake a knowledgeable person wouldn't make is a classic tell.

5. Image artifacts. Garbled text in the background, extra or fused fingers, jewellery that doesn't quite close, lighting that doesn't agree with itself.

6. No visible process. A finished piece with zero sketches, drafts, outtakes, or revision history behind it, where the creator's usual work does show that trail.

One of these is a maybe. Two or three together are worth logging in the field notebook below.
